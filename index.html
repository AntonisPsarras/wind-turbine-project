Wind Turbine Project

A 3D-printed balcony turbine that turns wind into charge for an 18650 battery. This guide covers the circuit, the perfboard layout, the soldering order and how the printed parts go together.

01

How it works

The blades spin a small DC motor, which then works as a generator. Its voltage goes up and down with the wind, so it passes through four stages before it reaches the battery.

Diodes
Charging

Illustrative simulation to show the behaviour, not measured data. Real voltages depend on your motor and the wind.

1 · Motor as generator

Spinning a brushed DC motor makes a DC voltage that rises with speed. If a gust pushes the rotor backwards, the polarity flips. Tick “Rotor spins backwards” to see it.

2 · Bridge rectifier

Four diodes make the output positive whichever way the motor turns, so the turbine wires can go either way round. Each pass costs two diode drops.

3 · Capacitor

A large electrolytic smooths gusts and the motor’s brush ripple, and gives the boost converter a short reserve to start from.

4 · MT3608 boost

Turns anything above about 2 V into a steady 5 V. Below that it does not start, which is why light wind gives no charge.

5 · TP4056 charger

Charges the 18650 to 4.2 V and protects it from overcharge, overdischarge and short circuit. Red LED is charging, blue is full.

03

Schematic

Two identical input channels feed one charger. Each MT3608 has its own output diode inside, so the two outputs can be joined directly.

B− is not ground on this charger. On the 03962A board the protection chip sits between B− and OUT−/IN−. Connect only the battery to B+/B−, and never join B− to any other negative wire, or the protection stops working.

    05

    Wiring the bridge rectifier

    This is where most mistakes happen. Build one bridge per turbine.

    The rule: every stripe faces the + rail. The stripe marks the cathode. Make two legs, each with two diodes in series, both stripes pointing up toward +.

    Motor wires go to the middle of each leg, one wire per leg. Polarity doesn’t matter here.

    The + rail (where two stripes meet) goes to the capacitor’s + leg and to VIN+ on the MT3608.

    The − rail (where two plain ends meet) goes to the capacitor’s stripe (−) and to VIN−.

    In the original, bridge A stands in a row at the bottom right with every stripe at the top, and bridge B is four diodes standing on end at the bottom left.

    Check before connecting the boost. Spin the motor by hand and measure between the rails. It should read positive either way you spin it.
    06

    Soldering, step by step

    Do the steps in this order. Each one ends with a quick check so a mistake can’t hide until the end.

    1. Set both MT3608 modules to 5.0 V before soldering anything

      Power each module’s VIN+/VIN− from 3.7–5 V (a USB lead or the 18650 itself). Measure VOUT and turn the brass screw on the blue trimmer until you read 5.0 V. It can take 10–20 turns before anything changes.

      New modules often arrive set to 12–28 V. The TP4056 is damaged above 8 V, so never connect a boost to the charger before setting it.
      VOUT = 4.9 – 5.2 V on both modules

    2. Mount the three modules

      Solder header pins into the module pads, then into the perfboard. From the top: boost B, the TP4056 with its USB socket at the left edge so you can still reach it, then boost A. On both boosts the VOUT pads face left and VIN pads face right, as in the photo.

      No pins bridged; modules sit flat

    3. Build bridge A and capacitor C1

      Four diodes in the lower right, stripes toward +, wired as in section 05. Put C1 across the + and − rails, stripe side to −.

      Spin turbine A by hand: rails read positive both ways

    4. Build bridge B

      Same circuit in the lower left. The original fits it in by standing the diodes on end. A second capacitor here is a good addition.

      Same test with turbine B

    5. Connect each bridge to its boost

      Bridge A + rail to boost A VIN+, − rail to VIN−. Bridge B to boost B the same way.

      Spin hard: boost output jumps to 5.0 V

    6. Join the outputs to the charger

      Both VOUT+ pads to TP4056 IN+. Both VOUT− pads to IN−. Keep these wires short, along the left edge.

      With a turbine spinning, IN+ to IN− = 5 V

    7. Add the input connectors

      Two 2-pin connectors on the bottom edge, wired to the middle points of each bridge. J1 (turbine A) is the centre one, J2 (turbine B) is on the left.

    8. Connect the battery last

      A 2-pin plug on the TP4056 B+/B− pads. Holder red lead to B+, black to B−. Check the polarity twice before inserting the cell. Cover every splice with heat-shrink.

      Reversed battery = dead charger, and possibly a hot cell. The TP4056 has no reverse-polarity protection.
      B+ to B− reads 3.0 – 4.2 V with the cell in

    9. Fit it in the box

      Battery holder on the left, board on the right, turbine leads out through the slots in the bottom. OUT+/OUT− stay free; that’s where a load such as a USB booster or a light can take power from the battery later.

    07

    Assembling the turbine

    Same order as the exploded view in the model files. Play it, or step through it part by part.

    Step 1 of 12

    Motor into the housing

      Wire routing

      Lead the two motor wires out under the nacelle and down beside the pole, as in the photos. Leave a loose loop at the top so the head can turn with the wind without pulling on the solder joints.

      Blade balance

      Weigh the three blades and sand the heaviest one until they match. An unbalanced rotor shakes, wastes energy and loosens the hub.

      Mounting

      The base clamps to a railing or post; the pin with the ring locks it. The originals also have a strap around the pole for extra hold in strong wind.

      Test on the bench with a cordless drill spinning the hub (gently, at low speed) before going outside.

      09

      Optional upgrades

      Not in the original build, but each one helps a small turbine.

      Schottky diodes

      Swap the 1N5408s for 1N5822. A silicon diode drops about 0.9 V, a Schottky about 0.4 V. Two sit in the path, so the turbine starts charging about 1 V sooner. Try it in the simulation above.

      Lower charge current

      The resistor marked R3 (printed “122”, 1.2 kΩ) sets 1 A of charge current, far more than a small turbine makes. Replacing it with 4.7 kΩ (about 250 mA) or 10 kΩ (about 130 mA) gives steadier charging.

      Second capacitor

      Add 1000 µF across bridge B as well, so both channels behave the same.

      Outdoor plastic

      PLA softens in summer sun on a balcony. PETG or ASA keep the blades and hub in shape.

      Keep the box cool and dry

      Li-ion cells shouldn’t charge above about 45 °C. Mount the box in shade and keep rain off the open front.

      Use the stored energy

      Connect a second MT3608 set to 5 V, or a small USB booster, to OUT+/OUT− to charge a phone or run a light from the battery.
